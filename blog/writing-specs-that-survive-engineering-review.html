Writing specs that survive engineering review

Most PRDs die the first time an engineer opens the architecture diagram. Here's the format I use so mine don't.

Aug 2026 · 6 min read · Drishti Chadha

I've sat on both sides of a spec review — as the person presenting it, and as the architect whose job is to find the hole in it. That second seat taught me more about writing PRDs than any template ever did. A spec that reads well in a stakeholder deck and a spec that survives an hour with a senior engineer are two different documents, and most PRDs are only written for the first audience.

Here's the gap I keep seeing, and the format I've settled on to close it.

The gap: specs describe outcomes, not constraints

A typical PRD says what should happen: "the system ingests customer data from three sources and produces a unified view." That's true, and it's also not enough for an engineer to size the work. They need to know: how large are the sources, how often do they change, what happens when a record fails validation, who owns the schema when it drifts. Leave those out and the spec doesn't get rejected outright — it gets silently re-scoped in a hallway conversation you weren't part of, and you find out about the real plan two weeks later.

A spec that only survives the stakeholder deck isn't a spec. It's a pitch wearing a spec's clothes.

What I put in every spec now

1. The constraint, stated as a number

Not "the data is large," but "82 tables, ~7.3GB, refreshed every 10 minutes via CDC." Every vague adjective in a spec is a question an engineer will have to ask you anyway — better to answer it before they have to interrupt their day to do it.

2. The failure path, not just the happy path

What happens on a schema change mid-migration? What happens if a source goes dark for six hours? If the spec doesn't say, the build either stalls on a design question or ships a silent gap. I list these explicitly, even when the answer is "out of scope for v1" — an intentional gap is fine; an invisible one isn't.

3. One diagram the engineer didn't have to draw themselves

A source-to-serving diagram, even a rough one, does more to align a review than three paragraphs of prose. It also surfaces disagreements early — if my diagram and the architect's mental model don't match, that's the actual conversation the review should be having, not a debate about roadmap priority.

4. Success criteria an engineer can test, not just a stakeholder can approve

"Improves reporting reliability" isn't testable. "99% of Gold-layer tables refresh within 15 minutes of source commit" is. If I can't write the criteria as something a test suite or a dashboard could check, I haven't finished defining the feature — I've just described the feeling I want it to produce.

Why this matters more in data and AI products specifically

In most product work, an ambiguous spec costs you a redesign. In a data platform, an ambiguous spec costs you a rebuilt pipeline, a re-migrated table, or a governance gap nobody notices until an audit. The blast radius of a vague requirement is bigger, and it's usually invisible until much later than a UI bug would be. That's the whole reason I write specs the way I do: not because engineers demand more rigor for its own sake, but because in this domain, the rigor is the risk mitigation.

The best compliment I get on a spec isn't "this is clear." It's "I didn't have to ask you anything."

That's the bar. Not a document that reads well once — one that still holds up after someone's spent an hour trying to poke a hole in it.
